## M01-01 The Anchoring Effect
Module: Bargaining & Anchoring
In one line: Whoever names a price first sets the tone (car haggling, salary negotiation)
Source: Tversky & Kahneman 1974
Boundary: Against a pro buyer holding a firm competing quote, the anchor barely grips — the first-move advantage nearly vanishes.

## M01-02 How Extreme an Anchor Can Go
Module: Bargaining & Anchoring
In one line: When to dare a bold ask — and when it blows up the deal
Source: Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001
Boundary: When they have endless alternatives and walking away costs them nothing, an extreme ask kills the deal before bargaining even starts.

## M01-03 Precise Numbers Anchor Harder
Module: Bargaining & Anchoring
In one line: The more precise the quote, the harder to haggle down (€14,875 holds firmer than €15,000)
Source: Loschelder et al. 2014
Boundary: Once they see through the "precision implies homework" trick and demand an item-by-item breakdown, the precise anchor collapses.

## M01-04 The Reciprocity Rule
Module: Bargaining & Anchoring
In one line: Every concession must buy something back
Source: Gouldner 1960
Boundary: When they read your concession as a calculated ploy rather than goodwill, it buys nothing — and reads as weakness.

## M01-05 Whoever's More Desperate Loses
Module: Bargaining & Anchoring
In one line: The side with the costlier clock concedes first
Source: Rubinstein 1982
Boundary: When they genuinely don't care whether the deal happens, you can't win a waiting game — change the chips instead.

## M01-06 The Repeated Game
Module: Bargaining & Anchoring
In one line: Never play ruthless with people you'll meet again (regulars, long-term suppliers)
Source: Axelrod 1984
Boundary: Against a stranger who vanishes after one deal, cooperation is charity — play the hard one-shot game.

## M02-01 Loss Aversion
Module: Persuasion Framing
In one line: People fear losing more than they crave gaining (insurance pitches, promo copy decoded)
Source: Kahneman & Tversky 1979
Boundary: When the loss is too small to feel — pocket change — or they've gone fully numb to it, the loss frame moves no one.

## M02-02 Reference Points
Module: Persuasion Framing
In one line: Same fact, different wording, different outcome — includes: spotting their reference point fast
Source: Kahneman & Tversky 1979
Boundary: When they catch you playing framing games and bristle, rewording only deepens the distrust.

## M02-03 The Desperate Gambler
Module: Persuasion Framing
In one line: Someone deep in the red dares bigger bets (the reflection effect — why loss-framing backfires on them)
Source: Kahneman & Tversky 1979
Boundary: If they still see one concrete path to recovery, loss-framing pushes them toward even bigger risks — first confirm they're truly out of options.

## M02-04 When Gain-Framing Wins
Module: Persuasion Framing
In one line: Tell someone in a hole what they can still save
Source: Tversky & Kahneman 1981
Boundary: When they're riding high and risk-averse, gain-framing makes them more conservative — use the loss frame to move them.

## M02-05 Turn It on Yourself
Module: Persuasion Framing
In one line: Write your goals as "what I'll lose by not acting"
Source: Kahneman & Tversky 1979（应用）
Boundary: When the goal is so big the "loss" is unimaginable (like health), loss-writing only manufactures anxiety — shrink the goal first.

## M03-01 The Zero-Base Question
Module: Sunk Costs & Strategic Quitting
In one line: "If I'd just inherited this project today, would I still fund it?"
Source: Arkes & Blumer 1985（应用）
Boundary: When the project carries irreversible reputational lock-in (a public promise), the honest answer warps — the exit cost isn't on the books, it's on your face.

## M03-02 Option Value
Module: Sunk Costs & Strategic Quitting
In one line: When to keep it alive instead of killing it (quitting vs. keeping the pilot light on)
Source: Myers 1977；Dixit & Pindyck 1994
Boundary: When keeping it alive costs more than the option itself is worth, "keeping the pilot light" is procrastination — kill it.

## M03-03 Pre-Commitment
Module: Sunk Costs & Strategic Quitting
In one line: Write the rules before you start
Source: Thaler & Shefrin 1981
Boundary: When the world changes so the rules' premises no longer hold, clinging to old rules is navigating today with yesterday's map.

## M03-04 Sunk Cost
Module: Sunk Costs & Strategic Quitting
In one line: For a trapped position, ask not "will it recover?" but "would I buy it today?"
Source: Arkes & Blumer 1985（应用）
Boundary: When the position carries an unreplicable information edge or tax/structural reasons, "would I buy today" can't directly dictate selling.

## M03-05 Sunk Costs in Careers & Relationships
Module: Sunk Costs & Strategic Quitting
In one line: Five years of experience vs. five years of shackles
Source: Arkes & Blumer 1985（应用）
Boundary: When those five years converted into portable assets (method, network), they're not shackles — tuition already paid.

## M03-06 Escalation of Commitment
Module: Sunk Costs & Strategic Quitting
In one line: The more you pour in, the harder it is to stop
Source: Staw 1976
Boundary: When new money genuinely changes the odds (a breakthrough actually near) rather than "one more bet," escalation doesn't apply — staying in is arithmetic.

## M04-01 The 2-Minute Start
Module: Beating Cognitive Friction
In one line: Just write the title, 120 seconds (dreaded tasks: expenses, data cleanup, routine reports — start first)
Source: Fogg 2009
Boundary: When the task's direction is wrong (strategy chaos), starting fast just walks you efficiently in the wrong direction — stop and decompose first.

## M04-02 Habit Stacking
Module: Beating Cognitive Friction
In one line: Weld the new move onto an old habit
Source: Gollwitzer 1999（应用）
Boundary: When the old habit itself is unstable (travel, broken sleep), the welded-on move breaks with it — fix the anchor first.

## M04-03 Environment Design
Module: Beating Cognitive Friction
In one line: Make starting easy, make distraction hard
Source: Thaler & Sunstein 2008
Boundary: When distraction comes from the task being meaningless rather than tempting surroundings, rearranging the room is useless — first answer "is this worth doing."

## M05-01 Why Bedtime Thinking Keeps You Awake
Module: Interrupting Rumination
In one line: The default-mode network idling at full speed
Source: Raichle et al. 2001
Boundary: When the anxiety hides one unexamined key risk, physical interruption is avoidance — write the risk down first.

## M05-02 Attention Restoration
Module: Interrupting Rumination
In one line: Change the scenery to recharge (walks and nature work best)
Source: Kaplan 1995
Boundary: When fatigue comes from decision exhaustion rather than monotony, walks recharge little — cut the decision load first.

## M05-03 Rumination vs. Review
Module: Interrupting Rumination
In one line: One spins its wheels, the other moves forward
Source: Nolen-Hoeksema 2000
Boundary: When it's not over and information is incomplete, forced "review" becomes convicting yourself on partial evidence — gather first.

## M05-04 Exercise as Circuit-Breaker
Module: Interrupting Rumination
In one line: Why your head clears after a run
Source: Blumenthal et al. 1999
Boundary: When the body is already overdrawn or injured, exercise deepens the deficit — sleep or stillness instead.

## M06-01 Consensus Is Priced In
Module: The Anti-Consensus Engine
In one line: An "opportunity" everyone knows about has no profit left by the time it reaches you
Source: Fama 1970
Boundary: Early, before the information has spread and consensus has formed, "priced in" doesn't apply yet.

## M06-02 The Mediocrity of Being Balanced
Module: The Anti-Consensus Engine
In one line: Why AI's "balanced view" can't guide action (an inference from RLHF mechanics)
Source: Christiano et al. 2017；Ouyang et al. 2022（推论）
Boundary: When the task only needs average (sorting info, running process), the balanced view is the efficient choice — don't burn compute hunting anti-consensus.

## M06-03 The Arms-Race Equilibrium
Module: The Anti-Consensus Engine
In one line: When everyone uses the same tool, the edge goes to zero (everyone writes résumés with AI; HR sees through all of them)
Source: Nash 1950/1951
Boundary: When how you use the tool has a know-how moat (prompt craft, domain experience), everyone having it does not equal everyone wielding it equally.

## M06-04 The Beauty Contest
Module: The Anti-Consensus Engine
In one line: Everyone's guessing not the truth but what others think
Source: Keynes 1936
Boundary: When every player is a fundamentals-driven long-term investor, the guessing-game layers don't apply — look at value itself.

## M06-05 Information Cascades
Module: The Anti-Consensus Engine
In one line: Rational individuals ditch private info to follow the crowd (the longest queue isn't the tastiest)
Source: Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch 1992
Boundary: When predecessors' actions carry verifiable hard info (published financials) rather than pure following, following is rational information use, not herd blindness.

## M06-06 Kill Your Own Consensus
Module: The Anti-Consensus Engine
In one line: Falsificationism — hand-kill one deeply held judgment every week
Source: Popper 1934/1959
Boundary: When the judgment itself is unfalsifiable ("tomorrow it goes up, down, or sideways" covers everything), forced falsification wastes a week — first check the proposition is well-formed.

## M07-01 Working Memory Limits
Module: Cognitive Science
In one line: You can juggle about 4 things in your head (7 to-dos in one meeting guarantees drops)
Source: Miller 1956；Cowan 2001
Boundary: Once information is chunked (a veteran hears one long number as a single unit), the 4-chunk cap doesn't bind — it limits chunks, not information.

## M07-02 Attention Residue
Module: Cognitive Science
In one line: The last task's thoughts follow you into the next one (after a heated meeting, every proposal looks wrong)
Source: Leroy 2009
Boundary: With a clear closing ritual between tasks (write the conclusion, shut the notebook), residue drops sharply — residue fears "unfinished," not "switching."

## M07-03 The Retrieval Practice Effect
Module: Cognitive Science
In one line: Before an exam, close the book and recite once — beats re-reading three times
Source: Roediger & Karpicke 2006
Boundary: When the knowledge never got in (you didn't understand it the first pass), forced recitation is carving on blank stone — understand first, retrieve second.

## M07-04 The Peak-End Rule
Module: Cognitive Science
In one line: People remember an experience by its peak and its ending (colonoscopy: long pain with a gentle ending gets rated "not that bad")
Source: Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier 1993
Boundary: When the rating comes from tallying a complete record (scoring against the full bill) rather than memory, peak-end doesn't apply — it governs "the remembered feeling," not "the computed total."

## M08-01 Regression to the Mean
Module: Statistical Thinking
In one line: This year's top scorer likely slips next year — not arrogance, just luck fading
Source: Galton 1886
Boundary: Doesn't apply across structural breaks (new coach, new rules, paradigm shifts) — the mean itself is drifting.

## M08-02 Base-Rate Neglect
Module: Statistical Thinking
In one line: Hearing "tested positive" and panicking, without asking how rare the condition is
Source: Kahneman & Tversky 1973
Boundary: When the base rate itself is unknown or shifting fast (early days of a new virus), "check the base rate first" has nothing to stand on — admit ignorance first.

## M08-03 Survivorship Bias
Module: Statistical Thinking
In one line: We only hear success stories, because failures never get book deals
Source: Wald 1943（二战飞机装甲，应用）
Boundary: When failed samples are available and included in the analysis, the bias is corrected — "beware survivorship bias" is not a universal shield.

## M08-04 The Law of Large Numbers
Module: Statistical Thinking
In one line: 7 heads in 10 flips is normal; 10,000 flips lands near half
Source: Bernoulli 1713
Boundary: When trials aren't independent (contagion, herding, feedback loops), the premise collapses — ask "independent?" before talking convergence.

## M10-01 The Michelson–Morley Experiment
Module: History of Science
In one line: A "failed" experiment that killed the aether
Source: Michelson & Morley 1887
Boundary: If "detecting nothing" came from instruments too crude to detect anything, a null result is not falsification — check precision before declaring death.

## M10-02 The Death of Phlogiston
Module: History of Science
In one line: Lavoisier weighed "phlogiston" out of existence
Source: Lavoisier 1770s
Boundary: Until the new theory's predictions become measurable numbers, don't rush to declare the old theory dead — build the scale first.

## M10-03 The Ulcer Bacteria Bet
Module: History of Science
In one line: Two doctors drank the bacteria to prove ulcers aren't "just stress"
Source: Marshall & Warren 1984
Boundary: When a self-experiment can't rule out placebo and individual variation, the conclusion is only "worth large-scale testing," not "proven" — drinking the bacteria is the starting line, not the finish.

## M11-01 The Dichotomy of Control
Module: Classical Decision Wisdom
In one line: Sort your worries into two piles — what I can move, what I can't
Source: Epictetus《手册》约108
Boundary: Fails when "only control the controllable" becomes an excuse to dodge necessary action ("the market's uncontrollable, so why review") — the first controllable item is the act of reviewing itself.

## M11-02 Premeditatio Malorum
Module: Classical Decision Wisdom
In one line: Spend a minute a day on "how bad could it get" — when it comes, you won't panic
Source: Seneca《致卢西利乌斯书信》约65
Boundary: Fails when rehearsal becomes looping the worst case on repeat, manufacturing chronic anxiety — rehearse once, write the plan, then stop. No reruns.

## M11-03 Amor Fati (Only for What's Done)
Module: Classical Decision Wisdom
In one line: Money already lost: call it tuition, not fate
Source: Marcus Aurelius《沉思录》约180（"amor fati"一词 Nietzsche 1882）
Boundary: Fails on what hasn't happened and can still change — resigning to the future is giving up; amor fati governs only the past.

## M11-04 Win First, Then Fight
Module: Classical Decision Wisdom
In one line: Before engaging, decide in advance where you'll admit defeat
Source: 《孙子兵法·形篇》约公元前5世纪
Boundary: Fails when "waiting for better odds" becomes never opening a position — waiting itself turns into an excuse for inaction; winning-first is preparation, not delay.

## M11-05 Prize Victory, Not Duration
Module: Classical Decision Wisdom
In one line: A battle you can't win only compounds losses the longer you drag it
Source: 《孙子兵法·作战篇》约公元前5世纪
Boundary: When dragging is accumulating advantage (waiting on earnings, on technical confirmation) and the cost is contained, "strike fast" is the wrong call — price the delay before calling the stop.

## M11-06 Know Where to Stop
Module: Classical Decision Wisdom
In one line: Before entering, decide: where you walk away in profit, where you walk away in loss
Source: 《道德经》第44章，约公元前4世纪
Boundary: When structural change voids the original exit terms — with a written revision on record — you may revise; the revision itself must name a new line in the sand. No verbal edits.

## M11-07 Wu Wei
Module: Classical Decision Wisdom
In one line: Don't predict; only respond
Source: 《道德经》第37/57章，约公元前4世纪
Boundary: When the variables are actually controllable and predictable (your own position, your own discipline), "wu wei" becomes an excuse for laziness — non-action applies only to the uncontrollable.
